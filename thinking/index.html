The End of Anonymous Creation

When AI participates in nearly everything, provenance becomes part of the artifact — not a disclosure buried in the footer, but part of what the thing actually is. This site now says, plainly, what I made and what I delegated. Here’s why that’s the right default, not just a nice one.

Dr. Paul X Claude ▾

AI-collaborative

The argument, the position, and every editorial decision are mine. Claude drafted language from my direction, and I revised it before it went live. That’s the tier this piece earns — see below for what the other two mean.

For most of the history of writing, the question “who made this” had a simple answer. A byline. A signature. Maybe an editor thanked in the acknowledgments. The work came from a person, and if it came from more than one person, you named them. That model is quietly breaking, and I don’t think most of us have caught up to how completely.

The old model was: author → content. The model most of us are actually operating in now looks more like: human → AI collaboration → artifact. Not because writers, marketers, and engineers have gotten lazier — because the tools got good enough that pretending they weren’t there stopped being honest. Every page on this site was written this way. The words you’re reading right now were drafted by Claude, from my direction, and edited by me before I let them go live. I don’t think that’s a confession. I think it’s just what happened, and saying so plainly is better than not saying it.

Provenance isn’t a disclaimer you add because the work might be less trusted. It’s information about the work — the same category as knowing who edited a film or which lab ran an experiment.

So I’m adopting a small standard for this site, and you’ll start seeing it show up: a mark, right near the content it describes, that says how the piece was made. Not buried in a footer as an afterthought — visible, and clickable if you want the detail. Three tiers, and I’m trying to be honest about which one actually applies rather than defaulting to whichever sounds best:

Human-authored. I wrote it. AI touched nothing, or close enough that crediting it would be noise.

AI-assisted. The thinking is mine — the argument, the structure, the conclusion. AI helped with editing, research, or turning notes into sentences faster than I would have alone.

AI-collaborative. AI materially participated in developing the thing itself — drafting language, proposing structure, writing code — under my direction, with my judgment deciding what stayed. Most of paulmress.com sits here, including this piece.

I want to be honest about why that last tier covers almost everything here: it does, and a badge that always says the same thing risks becoming wallpaper. I’d rather admit that up front than dress up the taxonomy to look more differentiated than my actual workflow is. Where the tiers will do real work is per-section, not per-page — which paragraph was mine outright, which decision Claude wasn’t in the room for. That’s a harder standard than a single site-wide badge, and it’s the one worth building toward.

This matters past my own site. The same question — what did the human contribute, what got delegated, which model, what sources — is going to come up in software, design, research, video, and especially marketing, which is the field I actually work in. A marketing org that can’t answer “which parts of this campaign were AI-generated and who approved them” isn’t practicing AI governance, no matter what the policy document says. Governance that only applies to other people’s work isn’t governance. It’s a talking point.

So this is me applying it to mine first, in public, where it’s easy to check. The mark above this paragraph isn’t a claim I’m making about the future of authorship. It’s just true, right now, about how this piece got made.
